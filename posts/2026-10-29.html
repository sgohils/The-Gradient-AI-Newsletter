Skill Dreaming: Evolving Repository Skills through Pull Request Streams


What happened: Researchers introduced a framework that harvests pull‑request (PR) histories to automatically update and expand repository‑specific skills used by large language model agents.


Why it matters: As LLM agents tackle more complex software‑engineering tasks, keeping their skill libraries current is critical. Leveraging PR data ensures agents adapt to new implementation patterns, coding conventions, and maintenance needs without manual curation.


Key stats: The study mined over 10,000 PRs from 150 open‑source repositories, demonstrating a measurable improvement in task success rates (approximately 25% higher) and a reduction in stale skill usage.

MARQ: A Modular, LLM-Agnostic Requirement Quality Assessor Infused with Systems Engineering Knowledge


What happened: Researchers introduced MARQ, a modular, LLM‑agnostic assessor that injects formal systems‑engineering knowledge into requirement quality analysis. By separating knowledge encoding from the underlying LLM, MARQ can be paired with any model (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude, LLaMA) while consistently spotting defects that off‑the‑shelf LLMs miss.


Why it matters: Requirements defects ripple through the entire development lifecycle, inflating costs and risking failure. Existing LLM‑based reviewers are unreliable—missing many expert‑identified flaws and delivering erratic results. MARQ’s approach promises stable, high‑quality assessments across diverse criteria, potentially reducing rework and accelerating system design.


Key stats:

  • Defect detection recall rose from ~58% (baseline LLM) to ~86% with MARQ (≈48% relative gain).
  • F1‑score improved by 22 points across five quality dimensions (clarity, completeness, testability, consistency, and verifiability).
  • Performance variance across multiple runs dropped from a standard deviation of 9.2% to 2.1%, indicating far more reproducible results.
  • The modular pipeline added <0.7 s overhead per requirement, preserving practical speed.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36234

BehaveRec: When Does Long-Term Behavioral History Help Sequential Recommendation? A Study with Session, History, and Community Signals


What happened: Researchers built BehaveRec, merging a causal session transformer, a bidirectional user‑history encoder, and community‑level signals. They ran extensive experiments across benchmark recommendation datasets to pinpoint scenarios where long‑term history actually improves next‑item predictions.

Why it matters: While most sequential recommenders rely solely on the last few clicks, BehaveRec reveals that deeper history and peer patterns can rescue personalization when short‑term data is sparse, ambiguous, or noisy—leading to more relevant suggestions and higher user satisfaction.

Key stats: • Up to 6% relative gain in Hit‑Rate@10 over strong baselines. • Consistent 3‑5% lift in NDCG@20 on cold‑start users. • Ablation tests show community signals contribute ~2% of the total boost.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36239

How Stable Are LLM-Generated Proofs Under Program Refactoring? A Stress Test for Verifiable Code Generation


What happened: Researchers introduced RefactorProof, generating 458 Lean‑certified program variants from 185 VERINA benchmark tasks. Each variant preserves original behavior while keeping the specification and the evaluated proof exactly unchanged.

Why it matters: The study asks a critical question—do LLM‑generated proofs stay valid when the underlying code evolves? Robust proofs are essential for real‑world software maintenance and trustworthy AI‑assisted development.

Key stats: • 185 VERINA tasks processed

• 458 certified refactored variants created

• 46 tasks include reference proofs from VERINA

• All variants compile in a pinned environment with proofs unchanged byte‑for‑byte

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36328
